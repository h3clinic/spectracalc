What the system does

Berlman's Handbook prints each compound's fluorescence and absorption spectra as ink curves inside a ruled box, with a wavenumber axis along the bottom and a wavelength axis along the top. Nothing about that page is machine-readable: there are no coordinates, no data, only ink. The system recovers the numbers by finding the box, reading the printed tick labels to calibrate the axes, following the ink, and pushing the traced pixels back through that calibration.

The whole chain is deterministic image processing — thresholds, morphology, robust line fits, column walks. There is no machine learning anywhere in it, including the "auto-trace" tool in the editors, which follows actual ink pixels between points a person clicks.

01

Source and rasterization

There is no cropping step, and that is a finding rather than an omission. The source is a single PDF of the Handbook in which each plate is already one page: pdfimages shows exactly one embedded image per graph page, 7457×4947, 1-bit, CCITT G4, at 800 ppi. The plates arrive pre-separated and pre-aligned, so any hand-rolled crop would only reintroduce registration error.

render_pages() shells out to poppler once for the whole book: 

subprocess.run(["pdftoppm", "-f", str(lo), "-l", str(hi), "-r", str(dpi),
                "-png", pdf, os.path.join(pages_dir, "graph")], check=True)

Rendering rather than extracting is deliberate. pdfimages would return the raw bilevel scan in its own coordinate frame, ignoring the page rotation and placement matrix; letting pdftoppm compose the page applies both uniformly, which is what makes 5400×3600 an invariant the rest of the system can assume. Downsampling 800 ppi to 600 also anti-aliases the 1-bit ink into a gray ramp — which is precisely what gives Otsu something to threshold.

Why pages 124–431

The page range is not a guess. pdfinfo reports the graph plates as the contiguous block of rotated pages: page 123 is unrotated, 124 through 431 are rotated, and 432 is unrotated again. That block is exactly 308 pages, matching the file count with no gaps. (290 are rotated 90° and 18 are rotated 270°; both render landscape.)

Why it matters

Page width is the sole input to _set_scale(), which sets a module-global _SCALE = width_px / 2700. Every pixel constant in the engine is authored at 300 DPI and multiplied up through _s(). If an unrotated page leaked into the range its width would be 3600 instead of 5400, _SCALE would come out 1.33 instead of 2.0, and every kernel and tolerance on that page would be silently a third too small.

Binarization

load_binary() is five lines: grayscale decode, then Otsu with inverted polarity so ink = 255. The threshold is recomputed per page — a fixed cut cannot survive 308 plates whose paper yellowing and exposure vary across the life of a 1971 print run. On graph-124 Otsu picks 132 with 2.78% ink; on graph-372, 135 with 2.25%.

02

Frame detection and axis calibration

detect_frame() recovers the plot box by morphological line extraction — the borders are the only long straight runs on the page, since curves aren't straight and text is short. Selection is region-based (one line per image half) rather than global, which is what survives a border partly broken where a curve crosses it.

The x-axis is then calibrated by OCR-ing the printed wavenumber tick labels and fitting pixel → wavenumber as a straight line. This is the single most consequential number in the system: it sets every wavelength that ever gets published.

The bug that shifted 26 pages

The fit is Theil–Sen, chosen for robustness against a misread tick. But SciPy's implementation anchors the intercept at median(y) − slope·median(x) — so a tick misread at the median position corrupts the intercept even though the slope survives. The trimming pass then made it worse: it tested residuals against zero rather than against the residual median, so it trimmed the good ticks and kept the bad one. Roughly 26 pages were silently shifted by hundreds to thousands of cm⁻¹ — the curves looked perfect, and every wavelength was wrong.

The fix re-centres the intercept on the median residual and trims around that median. Two guards were added on top: an RMSE gate that rejects any fit worse than 150 cm⁻¹, and a cross-check against the top wavelength axis (wavenumber = 10⁸/Å), with _calibrate_x_from_top() as fallback.

03

Curve isolation and tracing

isolate_curves() strips the frame, ticks, chemical structure diagram and legend text, leaving curve ink. Four tracing strategies then compete on every page: a skeleton-graph trace, an overlap-resolved variant of it, a columnar extractor, and a parallel-curve extractor.

Each candidate is scored by evaluate(), which rebuilds the traced curve into a mask and compares it against the isolated ink with a distance transform, tolerance _s(4) — 8 px at 600 DPI. That is what the reported F1 measures: agreement between the trace and the ink it came from, not agreement with any external truth. Strategy selection maximizes F1 + 0.3 × column_coverage, and if the best score falls below 1.2 the page is re-binarized under a sweep of adaptive and fixed thresholds and retried.

What F1 does and doesn't tell you

A high F1 means the trace sits on the printed line. It cannot detect a calibration error — a perfectly traced curve on a mis-calibrated axis scores just as well. Those two failure modes are independent, which is why calibration has its own gate and its own cross-check, and why the round-trip image exists.

04

Refinement passes

A raw trace is rarely publishable. The refinement chain runs in this order inside digitize(): _split_swallowed → _refine_curves → spike handling → _trace_dotted_tail → _label_guided_fix → _role_hygiene. Each pass exists because of a specific way pages fail.

PassWhat it fixesMechanism
_split_swallowedTwo mirror-image curves traced as oneFinds valleys below 0.09 intensity at least _s(12) wide with real mass on both sides, splits at the midpoint
_refine_curvesDropouts and stray pointsLifts points onto ink with a raw-pixel probe, fills gaps bidirectionally, prunes near-baseline clusters
_trace_dotted_tailTails printed as dots, not a lineMirror-slope projection, validated by requiring the end intensity below 0.10
_label_guided_fixA peak carved away by the legend textFires only when the raw maximum is under 0.9 and the carve-out peak reaches 0.9
_role_hygienePoints stolen across a crossingTwo wide-window (30 px) local-median passes cull points closer to the other curve's track, with an apex guard so needles survive

The apex guard is a good example of the tuning loop: the first version of _role_hygiene correctly removed a 15-point chord that had bled between curves on one page, and then removed a legitimate needle peak on another. The guard (y ≤ apex_y + _s(14)) keeps the cull from reaching the top of a curve.

05

From pixels to the published curve

Every traced dot is a pair of page pixels. The published curve is a monotone, single-valued series on a 0.1 nm grid. Four operations get from one to the other, in order.

1 · Pixel to wavenumber to wavelength

wn = xcal["a"] * px + xcal["b"]      # frozen per-page fit
wl = 1e7 / wn                        # cm⁻¹ → nm
inten = (y_bottom - py) / (y_bottom - y_top)

2 · Apex-local peak restoration

Berlman normalizes every spectrum to a peak of 1.0, so the digitized curve should too. The obvious approach — divide the whole curve by its maximum — is wrong, and visibly so: it lifts every point by the same factor, floating the entire trace roughly 1.5% above the printed ink. The round-trip image made that immediately obvious.

Instead only the top 2% band is stretched, so the peak reaches exactly 1.0 while the rest of the curve stays where the ink is: 

if 0.96 <= mx < 1.0:
    band_lo = mx - 0.02
    sel = inten > band_lo
    inten[sel] = band_lo + (inten[sel] - band_lo) * ((1.0 - band_lo) / (mx - band_lo))

3 · 0.1 nm dedup-averaging

Traced dots are per pixel column, and on a steep flank many columns land in one wavelength bin — so the raw series is multi-valued. Binning to 0.1 nm and averaging fixes that, with one exception: the bin holding the global peak keeps the apex value rather than the mean, or averaging would round the needle off and the peak would no longer be 1.0.

4 · Print-break bridging

Some plates have genuine breaks where the press left paper blank. Gaps up to 5.0 nm are linearly bridged so the exported curve is continuous. The ceiling is a hard cutoff — a 4.9 nm break is filled and a 5.2 nm one is not — and 24 of the 608 curves retain a gap above it, the largest 44.3 nm. All 608 are strictly monotonic in wavelength.

06

Validation

Validation runs on two independent axes, because the two failure modes are independent.

Round-trip: does the exported data land on the ink?

The final exported numbers — the same ones in the CSV and the Excel file — are pushed back through the inverse of the frozen calibration and replotted as dots on the raw scan. If the whole chain is sound the dots sit on the printed line; any wavelength error shows up as horizontal displacement. Measured on a sample page, the median gray level directly under a dot is 0 — pure ink — with 94% of emission and 91% of absorption dots landing on the line, the remainder on the thin flanks between.

This is the check that caught the global-rescale error, the colour mix-ups, and the calibration shifts. 305 of the 308 pages are under round-trip test; the 3 uncalibrated pages have nothing to replot.

External cross-check

Twelve compounds were compared against PhotoChemCAD reference spectra: emission mean r = 0.874 (n = 12), absorption mean r = 0.920 (n = 7), median peak difference 90 cm⁻¹. Benzene emission reached r = 0.9997 with zero peak offset.

Stale — flagged, not fixed

That cross-check was run against the first 300 DPI pass, whose mean F1 was 0.94. The shipped dataset is the 600 DPI regeneration at mean F1 0.973. Nothing in the repository currently cross-checks the numbers that actually ship against an external reference — the correlations above should be read as a floor, not as a measurement of the current data.

07

The two editors

Automation gets most pages right and cannot get all of them right. Both editors exist so a person can correct a curve directly on the scan, with every downstream artifact following.

Local editor — FastAPI, port 8770

Runs the real digitizer. Opening a page digitizes it (cached to disk, with an LRU that never evicts a hand-edited page), and Save writes the edit through the whole chain: the canonical dataset, the per-page CSVs, that compound's Excel workbook, the round-trip overlay, both viewers' inlined data, the editor's own cache, and a background rebuild of the master index. Every large write is atomic — tmp file plus rename — and a save that cannot reach any canonical copy returns an error rather than reporting success and republishing pre-edit data.

Browser editor — no server at all

The deployed site is static, so the browser editor cannot digitize anything. It doesn't need to: it reconstructs each dot by pushing the published numbers back through the frozen calibration — the same inverse transform that draws the round-trip image — and lays them over a clean page scan. Re-deriving data from those reconstructed dots returns the published numbers to floating-point epsilon (1.7×10⁻¹⁶).

Tools shared by both

  • Auto-trace — click a few points along missed ink and the run between them is followed pixel by pixel. Deterministic, no model. In the browser it walks an Otsu-thresholded bitmap of the scan; hops up to ~100 px land within 1% of the printed line 94–96% of the time, degrading to 76% at 400 px, so a long hop warns you.
  • Colour-scoped eraser — a capsule sweep that removes only the selected curve's colour, so a swipe through a crossing leaves the other spectrum untouched. Switchable to all-curves.
  • History — a snapshot timeline; any earlier state can be restored directly, and editing from an earlier step drops the abandoned future.
Why the browser tracer is the weaker one

The local app walks the isolated curve mask at full resolution. The browser walks a 2000-px-wide WebP of the whole page, so it sees the axes and legend text as ink too, and its stroke width is ~2 px rather than ~7. It compensates by following the curve's own trajectory and by penalizing the other curves' known tracks at crossings, but near-vertical flanks remain its weakest case.

08

Platform and data products

The public site is a static PhotochemCAD-style viewer: the whole dataset is inlined into one HTML file, charts are drawn on a device-pixel-ratio-scaled canvas, and the axis can be toggled between cm⁻¹ (as the book prints it) and nm. Ticks are anchored to round values and the y-axis is ruled every 0.1, so a reader can check the digitization against the scan above it.

The build shrinks the deliverable rather than the data: 308 round-trip overlays at 5400×3600 (~150 MB of PNG) become 2400-px WebP at 34.7 MB, and 308 clean scans become 22.2 MB. Per-compound data is split into individual JSON files so the editor fetches ~30 KB instead of an 11 MB blob.

Fixed during this audit

304 of the 308 per-compound workbooks were stale — written by an earlier regeneration on an integer-nm grid, while the CSVs were being generated fresh from the canonical dataset. The site was handing out a correct CSV and an outdated Excel file for the same compound. Worst case, graph-175 — a page whose calibration the pipeline rejects — still shipped a populated workbook of withheld data. Workbooks are now generated from the canonical dataset at build time, and the three uncalibrated pages produce no workbook at all.

Open issues found in this audit, not yet fixed

  • 18 secondary-emission curves are invisible in every tabular product. The em2 curves (20,717 points, on CURVE I/II comparison pages) exist only in the JSON — the CSVs, workbooks, master index and browser editor all handle just emission and absorption. A user working from Excel sees no trace of them.
  • Peak restoration is inconsistent between the two paths. The batch engine guards the apex stretch by role so em2 is never snapped; the app's version applies the same stretch with no role guard, so hand-editing an em2 curve can snap it to 1.0 and diverge from every batch-produced one.
  • Eight pages ship below F1 0.85 — 342, 380, 326, 373, 236, 170, 222, 200 — and only 342 appears in the published limitations table.
  • The README's coverage numbers are one regeneration stale (it claims 306 emission / 286 absorption and names two uncalibrated pages; the shipped figures are 305 / 285 and three).
  • The batch regeneration and evaluation scripts are not in the repository. They lived in session scratch space. The protocol survives as prose and the per-page round-trip generator survives inside the save path, but the batch run cannot currently be reproduced from a clean checkout.
